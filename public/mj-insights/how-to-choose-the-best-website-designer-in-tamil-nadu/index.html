1. Start with the job your website must do

Before contacting designers, write down the main purpose of the website. A service business may need clear service pages and enquiry forms. A retailer may need product categories and online checkout. A professional firm may need credibility, team information and appointment enquiries. Your purpose determines the scope.

2. Review real, relevant portfolio work

Look beyond screenshots. Open the websites on a phone and desktop. Check whether the pages load, navigation works, contact details are easy to find and the design suits the business. Ask what work the designer completed and whether the examples are live. A portfolio should help you judge relevant experience, not just visual taste.

3. Compare scope, not just price

Ask each provider for a written list of deliverables: number and type of pages, custom design, responsive layouts, content entry, forms, integrations, basic technical SEO, analytics setup, testing, launch and training. Two quotes are not comparable if one includes content and testing while another only includes a template and installation.

4. Check mobile experience and accessibility

Most customers may first see your website on a phone. Check readable text, tappable buttons, simple menus, image sizing, form usability and contrast. Ask how the designer tests different screen sizes. Important information should not be hidden behind hover-only interactions.

5. Ask about SEO foundations

A designer should understand crawlable links, unique page titles, useful descriptions, heading structure, image alternative text, canonical URLs, sitemap availability, indexability and page performance. These foundations help search engines understand a site, but no designer can guarantee a first-page ranking.

6. Clarify ownership and ongoing support

Confirm who owns the domain, hosting account, website files, source code, images, fonts and licences. Ask how you will receive administrator access and backups. Get clear terms for maintenance, updates, security fixes, renewal charges and support response times.

7. A simple comparison framework

Score each proposal against business understanding, portfolio relevance, scope clarity, mobile quality, SEO fundamentals, ownership, communication and after-launch support. A slightly higher quote may represent better value if it includes the work your business actually needs.

8. Questions to ask before signing

Ask: What exactly is included? Who writes the content? Are images licensed? Is the site responsive? Will I own the domain and files? What happens if I want to change providers? Are hosting and renewals separate? How many revision rounds are included? What is the testing and launch process?

9. A sensible project workflow

A professional process usually moves from discovery and scope to sitemap, content, design, development, testing, launch and post-launch review. You should know what decisions are needed from you at each stage and who approves the final work.

Practical next stepUse the points above as a checklist. Prioritise the issues that affect your customers first, record what you changed and review the result rather than assuming every change will produce an immediate ranking or sales improvement.

Frequently asked questions

Should I choose a freelancer or an agency?

Choose based on relevant work, communication, capacity, process and support—not the label. A capable freelancer may suit a small project; a team may be useful when design, development, content and marketing need coordinated work.

How long does a business website take?

It depends on page count, content readiness, integrations, feedback and approval speed. Ask for milestones and dependencies rather than relying on a generic timeline.

Can a web designer guarantee Google rankings?

No. Search rankings depend on many factors outside a designer's control. A provider can implement sound technical foundations and help you create useful content, but should not promise a specific position.
