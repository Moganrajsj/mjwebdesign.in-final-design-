1. First understand crawling, indexing and ranking

Crawling is when Googlebot fetches a page. Indexing is when Google analyses and may store it in Google's index. Ranking is the process of choosing results for a particular search. A page can be crawled but not indexed, indexed but rank too low to be noticed, or not yet discovered at all.

2. Check whether the homepage or URL is indexed

Search Google for site:yourdomain.com as a rough check, then use Search Console's URL Inspection tool for a more reliable URL-specific status. Search operators do not show every indexed URL. Inspect the exact canonical URL you want Google to show.

3. Check the technical requirements

Google's technical requirements include that Googlebot is not blocked, the page returns a successful HTTP response and the page has indexable content. Check that the page is public, returns HTTP 200, and does not accidentally include a noindex directive.

4. Review robots.txt and noindex settings

A robots.txt rule can prevent crawling. A meta robots noindex tag or X-Robots-Tag header can prevent indexing when Google can crawl the page and read the directive. These controls are different: blocking crawling can prevent Google from seeing a noindex directive. Review both carefully before changing them.

5. Check canonical URLs and duplicate pages

A canonical tag indicates the preferred version among duplicate or similar URLs, but Google may choose a different canonical if its signals disagree. Ensure internal links, sitemap URLs and redirects consistently point to the preferred HTTPS and hostname version.

6. Make sure Google can discover the page

Add useful internal links from relevant pages using normal crawlable anchor links. Include important canonical URLs in the XML sitemap. A sitemap helps Google discover URLs but does not guarantee crawling or indexing. Avoid orphan pages that have no internal links.

7. Improve the page's actual value

Google does not index every page it crawls. Thin, duplicated, automatically generated or unhelpful pages may not be selected. Make each page meaningfully useful, specific, original and clear about who it serves. Avoid creating many near-identical location pages just to capture searches.

8. Check server and rendering problems

Look for 4xx and 5xx errors, redirect loops, blocked CSS or JavaScript resources, slow or unstable hosting and content that appears only after user interaction. Use URL Inspection's live test and inspect the rendered result when available.

9. A practical troubleshooting sequence

1. Confirm the exact URL is public. 2. Inspect it in Search Console. 3. Check HTTP status and redirects. 4. Review robots.txt and noindex. 5. Check canonical selection. 6. Add internal links and sitemap entry. 7. Improve content where needed. 8. Request indexing after fixing the issue, then monitor the report.

10. What not to do

Do not repeatedly submit the same unchanged URL, buy indexing guarantees or create doorway pages. Do not remove noindex or robots rules blindly if they protect private or duplicate content. Fix the cause first and keep a record of the change.

Practical next stepUse the points above as a checklist. Prioritise the issues that affect your customers first, record what you changed and review the result rather than assuming every change will produce an immediate ranking or sales improvement.

Frequently asked questions

How long does Google take to index a page?

There is no guaranteed timeline. Crawling and indexing can take days or longer, and requesting indexing does not guarantee inclusion.

Does submitting a sitemap guarantee indexing?

No. A sitemap helps Google discover URLs but does not guarantee that Google will crawl or index them.

Why does Search Console say indexed but I cannot find my page?

The page may not rank prominently for your query, Google may show a different canonical URL, or the query may not be relevant enough. Search Console URL Inspection and Performance reports help diagnose the difference.
